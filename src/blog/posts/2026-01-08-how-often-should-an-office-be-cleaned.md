There is no single cleaning frequency that suits every office. A quiet professional practice with six desks has different demands from a customer-facing workplace with shared kitchens, washrooms and a steady flow of visitors.

The useful question is not simply “How many cleans do we need?” It is: **which areas need attention, how often, and what standard should each visit achieve?**

This guide provides a practical starting point for Derby businesses planning a reliable office cleaning schedule.

## Start with how the workplace is actually used

Before choosing a frequency, look at the factors that create cleaning demand:

- Number of employees and average daily attendance
- Hybrid-working patterns and busiest weekdays
- Visitors, customers or members of the public
- Number and type of washrooms
- Whether staff prepare meals in the kitchen
- Hard flooring, carpets and entrance matting
- Shared desks, meeting rooms and touchpoints
- Opening hours and suitable access times
- Seasonal mud, rain and grit entering the building

A smaller office may need only two or three scheduled visits each week if staff attendance is low. A busy site with shared facilities may need cleaning every working day, with washrooms and kitchens checked more than once.

## Tasks that often need daily attention

Daily cleaning should concentrate on hygiene, presentation and anything that quickly becomes unpleasant.

Typical daily tasks include:

- Emptying general waste and replacing liners
- Cleaning toilets, basins, taps and washroom touchpoints
- Wiping kitchen worktops, sinks and appliance handles
- Vacuuming or mopping busy entrances and walkways
- Spot-cleaning fingerprints from doors and glass
- Wiping shared tables and meeting-room surfaces
- Replenishing soap, toilet tissue and paper products
- Reporting leaks, damage or unexpectedly low supplies

If the office is only partly occupied on certain days, the checklist can follow usage rather than treating every day identically. For example, the cleaner can prioritise meeting rooms after client days and give kitchens extra attention after team events.

## Tasks suited to a weekly rotation

Some work does not need completing during every visit, but it should not be left indefinitely. A weekly rotation prevents dust and grime building up in less obvious places.

Consider scheduling:

- Detailed dusting of desks, ledges and accessible shelving
- Cleaning internal glass and partition marks
- Wiping skirting boards and low-level surfaces
- Vacuuming edges and beneath movable furniture
- Cleaning microwave interiors and cupboard fronts
- Descaling washroom fixtures where required
- Dusting vents, radiators and window sills
- Cleaning low-use rooms and storage areas

Rotational tasks should appear on the written specification. If they are merely described as “when there is time”, urgent daily jobs will usually push them aside.

## Periodic cleaning protects the workplace

Routine visits maintain a site, while periodic work resets areas that regular cleaning cannot fully address.

Depending on the office, periodic tasks may include:

- Carpet extraction cleaning
- Upholstery cleaning
- Hard-floor machine cleaning or refinishing
- High-level dusting
- Detailed kitchen and appliance cleaning
- Internal window cleaning
- Deep cleaning washrooms
- Cleaning behind and beneath heavy furniture

Plan these jobs in advance—often quarterly, six-monthly or annually—rather than waiting until an area looks noticeably tired. The right interval depends on footfall, floor type and the impression the workplace needs to create.

## A simple frequency guide

Use this as a starting point, then adjust it after observing the site:

| Workplace pattern | Starting schedule |
| --- | --- |
| Small, low-occupancy office | Two or three visits per week |
| Medium office with shared kitchen and washrooms | Cleaning every working day |
| Customer-facing or high-footfall site | Daily cleaning plus daytime checks where needed |
| Hybrid office | Visits concentrated around peak attendance days |
| Multi-tenant communal areas | Daily or usage-led checks with clear responsibility |

This is a planning guide, not a fixed rule. A site assessment should confirm the appropriate time and task allocation.

## Signs the current schedule is too light

Your office may need more time, more frequent visits or a better task split if:

- Bins overflow before the next clean
- Washroom supplies repeatedly run out
- Kitchen surfaces remain marked during working hours
- Entrance floors look poor by midday
- Dust accumulates on ledges and equipment
- Staff regularly raise the same cleaning concerns
- Cleaners cannot complete the agreed checklist within the visit

Increasing frequency is not always the only answer. Sometimes the solution is clearer scope, better equipment, improved storage or moving periodic tasks away from routine visits.

## Turn the schedule into a measurable specification

A dependable contract should state the rooms covered, tasks, frequencies, access arrangements, consumables responsibility and reporting process. Agree how issues are raised and how the schedule will be reviewed when staffing or building use changes.
