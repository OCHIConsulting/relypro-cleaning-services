A turnover cleaner may be the first person to notice a chipped glass, a leaking tap or a shortage of fresh towels. The value of that observation depends on how clearly it reaches the host and whether someone makes a timely decision.

For Derby hosts and property managers, a reporting process should answer three questions: what has been found, how does it affect the next arrival and who needs to act? It should not require cleaners to investigate disputes or decide who caused damage.

## Agree what needs immediate contact

Some findings can wait for a completion summary; others need a call before work continues. Set these categories before the first turnover so the cleaner does not have to guess under pressure.

An access failure, suspected safety issue or unusable essential facility needs prompt escalation through the agreed route. Staff should follow their training and avoid handling hazards outside their competence.

A low supply of spare bin liners is a different kind of problem. It still needs an owner, but it should not obscure an urgent issue in a long list of minor shortages.

## Write observations rather than accusations

A useful report describes the item and location, then the visible condition. “Bedroom two bedside lamp does not switch on” is more actionable than “guest broke the lamp”.

Include the time the issue was observed and whether cleaning can continue safely. Do not move or dismantle equipment to prove a cause unless that action is authorised and appropriate.

| Report field | Example of useful detail |
| --- | --- |
| Location | Main bathroom, beneath basin |
| Observation | Water visible in cupboard |
| Readiness impact | Storage area cannot be reset |
| Action requested | Manager to arrange assessment |
| Current status | Reported; awaiting instruction |

These are illustrative examples, not records of a RelyPro customer property. Adapt the fields to the information the manager actually needs.

## Make photographs purposeful

Take a context image showing where the issue is, followed by a closer image if needed. Clear lighting and a simple description often help more than several near-identical photographs.

Avoid including guest identification, correspondence, screens, faces or security codes. Check mirrors and other reflections before sharing. Store and share photographs using the agreed business process, with access restricted appropriately.

Do not post property issues to public channels. Cleaning completion evidence and a guest-facing property listing serve different purposes.

## Report stock in quantities

“Low toiletries” gives little indication of what to buy. Name the item, quantity available and agreed minimum. Identify whether the stock is on site but inaccessible, or genuinely unavailable.

Separate consumables from reusable inventory. Missing teaspoons, a shortage of clean linen and low washing-up liquid may need three different responses.

Agree whether the cleaning team can use reserve stock or purchase replacements, and what approval is needed. Avoid assuming that a cleaner has authority to spend on the host's behalf.

## Confirm the outcome

A report is not complete simply because it has been sent. The manager should acknowledge urgent issues, make the decision and tell the cleaner what to do next. Record whether the property is ready, ready with an agreed exception or awaiting action.

Review repeated shortages and maintenance reports periodically. They may reveal a stock-planning problem or a repair that has repeatedly been deferred.
