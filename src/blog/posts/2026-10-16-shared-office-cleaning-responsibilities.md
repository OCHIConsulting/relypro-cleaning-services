In a shared office, a missed task can belong to several people in theory and nobody in practice. Staff may assume the cleaner washes cups, the cleaner may be instructed to avoid desk contents and the landlord may only cover common corridors.

A simple responsibility map helps everyone understand what happens during the day and what is included in scheduled cleaning. For Derby workplaces sharing a building or facilities, start by checking existing agreements before creating new expectations.

## Draw the boundaries of the space

List the areas your organisation controls and those shared with other occupiers. Include entrances, stairs, toilets, kitchenettes, meeting rooms and waste storage.

Confirm who manages each shared area. A landlord's service arrangement may differ from the cleaning commissioned by an individual office tenant. Do not assume that one provider can enter or work in every room.

Keep the boundaries in a short document available to the office contact and cleaning team. Update it when rooms change use or another occupier joins.

## Separate tidying from cleaning

A desk covered with papers cannot be treated like an empty worktop. Agree whether cleaners should leave items undisturbed and what staff need to clear beforehand.

Washing up, emptying personal food containers and moving confidential documents also need explicit ownership. The task may be simple, but it still consumes time and may create privacy or access concerns.

| Task | Responsibility to agree |
| --- | --- |
| Personal desks | Staff preparation and cleaner access |
| Shared kitchen | Staff use, daily reset and scheduled clean |
| Meeting rooms | Crockery removal and surface cleaning |
| Communal toilets | Building provider or tenant arrangement |
| Waste | Internal bins, collection point and removal |

Use named roles where possible. “Office coordinator” or “building manager” is more durable than relying on one person who may be absent.

## Plan for issues between visits

A cleaning visit cannot cover every spill that happens during working hours. Establish how staff report a wet floor, overflowing bin or empty washroom supply.

The response should fit the issue and workplace arrangements. Staff should not be expected to handle hazardous substances or equipment without suitable instruction.

Keep agreed supplies accessible to those authorised to use them. Products should remain labelled and stored appropriately; a cupboard full of unmarked bottles makes a simple response harder and less safe.

## Protect information and equipment

Discuss screens, keyboards and specialist equipment separately from general furniture. Follow the relevant manufacturer's instructions and agree who is authorised to clean sensitive equipment.

Do not ask cleaners to move confidential files to complete an ordinary surface clean. A clear-desk routine can improve access without transferring responsibility for information security to the cleaning team.

Access instructions should also specify restricted rooms, alarm arrangements and the contact for an unexpected issue. Share sensitive details through the approved secure route.

## Review the agreement when occupancy changes

More staff, different working days or regular events can change cleaning needs. A meeting room used once a week may require a different routine when it becomes a daily training space.

Use a short monthly review of recurring exceptions to decide whether responsibilities or frequency need adjustment. Record changes so the provider is working to the current arrangement.
