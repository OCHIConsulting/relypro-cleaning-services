An office kitchen can look tidy while sticky handles, food residue and forgotten fridge items continue to build up. The solution is a clear division between what staff do after using the space and what the cleaning service completes on its agreed visits.

For a small Derby workplace, start with the busiest periods: morning drinks, lunch and the end of the working day. A schedule based on actual use will be more practical than treating every kitchen as though it has the same number of users.

## Give everyday tasks a clear owner

Staff should know where used crockery goes, how spills are handled and when personal food must be removed. A cleaning contract should state whether washing up or loading a dishwasher is included; neither should be assumed.

Place waste and recycling containers where they are easy to use without blocking access. Make the arrangement simple enough for visitors and temporary staff to understand. If one bin repeatedly overflows before the cleaner arrives, change the collection routine rather than relying on reminders.

## Use a daily close-down checklist

The person completing the agreed kitchen clean can work from higher surfaces to lower areas, leaving the floor until last. Use products suitable for each surface and follow their instructions, including any rinsing requirements for food-contact areas.

- Remove waste and fit fresh liners where needed.
- Clear accessible worktops before cleaning them.
- Clean the sink and taps, including visible residue around fittings.
- Wipe appliance handles and exterior controls.
- Check splashbacks and the table for spills.
- Clean the floor and leave it safe to use.

Keep cloths and equipment for toilets separate from those used in the kitchen. A colour system can help, but staff still need to understand the separation and how equipment is cleaned and stored.

## Put appliance interiors on the schedule

A microwave interior needs attention when food splashes occur, while fridge shelves require an organised approach to removing contents. Agree who is allowed to discard food and how staff will be notified before a planned fridge clean.

| Task | Agree before cleaning |
| --- | --- |
| Fridge interior | Food removal, timing and who authorises disposal |
| Microwave | Safe isolation and manufacturer instructions |
| Dishwasher | Filter care and responsibility for loading |
| Kettle | Suitable descaling method and rinsing |
| Cupboards | Whether contents must be moved by staff |

Do not spray directly into electrical equipment or immerse components that are not designed for it. Follow the appliance manufacturer's cleaning instructions rather than improvising with stronger products.

## Check the things people touch

Cupboard pulls, fridge handles, chair backs and switches are easily overlooked. Include them explicitly in the checklist so the cleaner does not have to choose between visible presentation and frequently used points.

Where disinfection is appropriate, the product needs its stated contact time. Immediately wiping it away may not achieve the intended result. Cleaning and disinfection are separate steps unless the product instructions specify a combined method.

## Review recurring problems

A weekly spot check can focus on worktops, bins, appliance handles and the sink. Record exceptions in plain language: for example, food left on worktops prevented cleaning, or the bin needed an additional collection.

Repeated issues often concern responsibilities rather than effort. Revise the staff routine or cleaning scope when use changes, especially after recruiting more people or introducing shared lunches.
